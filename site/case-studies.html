Case studies

Six worked examples, all on Halden Logistics, a fictional company. Nobody here is a real client, and none of these outcomes happened. Each one runs the kit's own demo data through a tool or template, so you can check every number against the files, and each ends with how you would prove the benefit.

How to read these

These are worked examples, not client case studies. Halden is a fictional company, and its data lives in this repo's demo estate, with problems planted on purpose. Each case covers what the records showed, what the kit's tool did with them, which decisions stayed with people, and what a real team would still have to check before trusting the result.

Every point where a person decides is marked Decision needed:, the same way the tools mark it. Nothing here is legal advice; contract and audit questions go to counsel. Each case links to its tool card, the full Halden example the tool produced, and the matching practicum exercise. Work the exercise by hand first if you want an honest test of yourself, then compare.

Each case ends with how you would prove the benefit. The rules are the same every time. Measure a baseline from the files on a named date. Count a benefit only after you measure again and keep the evidence. Keep forecasts apart from achieved results, and net out any cost the change adds. Write Not found where the files hold no figure. Halden's outcomes are illustrative, because the company is fictional; the method is what carries over to your own estate.

Audit response: a publisher letter and what the records could prove

The short answer, before anyone replied: even counts on paper for Shapewise and both Cellmate versions, 17 named users short on Taskmoor, three proofs needing repair and one needing an explanation. The records could not settle whether the publisher holds an audit right at all; that belongs to counsel.

The situation

Brightvale Software Group wrote to Halden on 2026-08-24 about three product lines it bought from Mosswood Software in 2025: Taskmoor, Shapewise, and Cellmate. It asks for installs at every Halden group company in any country, records back to 2019, proof of purchase, a device-level list with user names, and a reply within 30 days. General counsel asks the asset manager what the records can prove before anyone replies.

What the records showed

Six proofs of purchase, four install counts, and Halden's own notes on its 2022 agreement (HAL-MSW-2022). The notes license Taskmoor and Shapewise per named user and Cellmate per device, and that changes the math. Count raw installs and Shapewise looks 12 short (297 against 285 proofs); count assigned users and it is even. Taskmoor is the real gap: P-001 and P-002 cover 272 named users against 289 assigned. As for the proofs, P-003 has no buyer name, P-004 has no payment proof, and P-006 upgrades from a Cellmate version 1 that Halden holds no proof of. P-005 was first ordered on 2022-05-04, after the first version 2 install on 2022-03-01. A true-up, or a later purchase?

What the tool did

The audit response check reconciled each product and version in its own license metric, read every proof for the details it must show, checked the upgrade's base, and compared proof dates with first installs. It then set the letter beside Halden's unverified notes, which limit the review to Halden Logistics Inc, three products, use in the 36 months before the notice, and counts per product and version. The letter asks for more on all four. Output: a briefing for Legal and the executive sponsor, and a list of don'ts. No contact with the publisher, no removing Taskmoor installs, no device list unless counsel releases one.

The decisions that stayed with people

  • Decision needed: counsel. Was the Mosswood agreement assigned to Brightvale, and does it give Brightvale an audit right?
  • Decision needed: counsel. Whether to push back on the letter's scope, what to release, and when.
  • Decision needed: counsel. Whether downgrade rights let a version 3 license cover version 2 installs.
  • Decision needed: the executive sponsor, with counsel, on any purchase to close the Taskmoor gap.

What a real team would still check

The audit clause, the license metrics, and the accepted forms of proof came from Halden's notes, not the signed contract; the tool marked them unverified. A real team pulls the signed text first, then chases P-003's buyer name, P-004's payment record, and a version 1 purchase behind P-006. If those don't turn up, Shapewise and Cellmate version 3 both move from even to short. And nobody uninstalls Taskmoor from 17 users to make the count look clean unless counsel agrees.

The benefit, and how to prove it

  • Measure: Proofs of purchase with no open issue, and named users short on Taskmoor.
  • Baseline in the files: 2 of 6 proofs have no open issue (three need repair, one needs an explanation), and Taskmoor is 17 named users short.
  • Target: 6 of 6 proofs complete and accepted, and a Taskmoor position that is even or explained.
  • Evidence to keep: The located buyer name, payment record, and version 1 purchase; the reconciliation per product and version; and counsel's decisions on what was released.
  • Forecast or achieved: Not yet proven. Any cost avoided by answering from prepared evidence is Not found in the files, so no money figure is claimed. The measure is the share of proofs that stand up.

Record it in the benefits tracker and measure again after the change.

Tiering an AI use case: the CV ranking tool

AI-004, an HR tool that ranks job applicants' CVs, was the only one of Halden's six AI systems tiered high-risk. Review changed the legal detail around it, not the tier.

The situation

Halden has six AI systems with six different owners, and nobody has ever tiered any of them for risk. The AI governance board is about to meet for the first time, and someone has to say which system, if any, must stop for legal review first. The inventory gives each system an id, an owner, and one line of description. That's all.

What the records showed

Most of the list is routine: a spam filter, invoice extraction that a person reviews, route suggestions a dispatcher approves, a shipment-status chatbot, and a volume forecast. AI-004 is different. HR uses it to rank applicants' CVs and shortlist candidates for interview. The record doesn't say whether a person reviews every ranking, who supplies the tool, or what data trained it.

What the tool did

The AI intake tool restated each use case, checked prohibited practices first, then the high-risk areas. Ranking job applicants is an employment use, and employment is one of those areas. The tool weighed the exception for narrow procedural tasks and rejected it, because ranking applicants is profiling people. Result: high-risk, legal review before approval. On top of the baseline every system gets, it required recruiters who can override rankings and see who wasn't shortlisted, notice to applicants, bias and accuracy monitoring, kept logs, supplier conformity evidence, a data protection impact assessment, and a risk register entry owned by the Head of HR. The chatbot came out limited-risk because it talks to the public; the other four came out minimal.

The decisions that stayed with people

A reviewer read the output on 2026-09-24 and corrected three things. The tool had treated a change to the high-risk deadline as a pending proposal when it had already been adopted. It named the wrong party as bound by the chatbot disclosure duty. And it cited a whole article where one paragraph applied. None of that moved the tier, which came from what the system does. All of it is legal detail, which is why legal review is on the list.

  • Decision needed: legal review. Confirm the tier, whether Halden is a deployer or a provider, that no emotion-recognition or biometric feature exists, and when the obligations apply.
  • Decision needed: approval with a named approver. Nothing deploys before both legal review and approval.
  • Decision needed: the review cycle. The tool suggested every six months; at minimum yearly, and on any change of model or use.

What a real team would still check

Whether a person actually reviews every ranking, since the description doesn't say. Which product this is: the tool's guess, Hirewell Recruiting (APP-004), is marked unverified and should stay that way until HR confirms it. What the vendor's model was trained on. And the current official timeline for high-risk obligations, which had already moved before this example ran.

The benefit, and how to prove it

  • Measure: AI systems with a recorded risk tier and a named owner.
  • Baseline in the files: 6 of 6 systems have a named owner. 0 of 6 have a tier.
  • Target: 6 of 6 tiered, with legal review and a named approver recorded for AI-004 before it is approved.
  • Evidence to keep: The inventory rows with tier, date and reviewer; the legal review record; the approval; and the risk register entry for AI-004.
  • Forecast or achieved: A risk benefit, not a money one. No cost figure is claimed, and none is Found in the files.

Record it in the benefits tracker and measure again after the change.

Rationalization: two transport systems, one job

Halden runs two transport management systems. The tool recommended keeping Routesmith and retiring Routewise, and put the savings across all five duplicate pairs at $250,000 to $355,400 a year. That range is a forecast, not money in the bank.

The situation

Finance wants a savings estimate for next year's budget cycle. The CIO wants to know which applications duplicate each other before that number goes anywhere. The inputs are an inventory of 60 applications (category, annual cost, users, data sensitivity) and a license file with one license per application, its entitlements, its expiry, and the date it was checked, 2026-09-01.

What the records showed

Grouping by category instead of by name turns up five pairs doing the same job. The transport pair is the starkest: Routewise (APP-029) costs $54,700 a year for 193 users, $283.42 per user; Routesmith (APP-058) costs $14,300 for 366 users, $39.07 per user. Two licenses had expired before their own checked-on date: LIC-017 for Meetrix video conferencing (2026-03-31, 649 users) and LIC-044 for Payflow accounts payable (2025-12-31, 748 users, confidential data). Neither belongs to a duplicate. The inventory also has no owner column, so none of the 60 apps has a confirmed owner.

What the tool did

The rationalization tool gave every app a TIME call: tolerate, invest, migrate, or eliminate. With technical fit poor on both sides of every pair, it broke the tie on cost per user and marked the pricier side Eliminate: APP-008, APP-022, APP-029, APP-033, and APP-052, $355,400 a year combined. It marked both expired-license apps Invest, meaning renew; the tool treats a lapsed contract on a busy app as a renewal, not a retirement; how many seats to renew, and any use since the expiry, go to the contract owner. It gave savings as a range. The high end assumes clean, immediate exits. The low end is a judgment about notice periods and wind-down eating into year one, not a calculation from the files, and no contract terms were supplied to test either end.

The decisions that stayed with people

  • Decision needed: app owners and architecture confirm Routewise has no dependency Routesmith can't cover. The same goes for the other four pairs.
  • Decision needed: an owner named for each app before any Eliminate call is carried out.
  • Decision needed: the data owner sets disposition and retention for each retiring app, such as moving Routewise's open routes to Routesmith.
  • Decision needed: renewing LIC-017 and LIC-044. The tool recommends it; someone with the budget approves it.

What a real team would still check

Whether cost per user is a fair tie-break at all. The users column isn't documented as licensed seats or installs, so the per-user figures are rough. Routesmith's license shows 279 entitlements against 366 users today; moving Routewise's 193 users onto it is a license question before it is a saving. Notice periods and penalties on the five retiring contracts decide where in the range the real number lands. One more thread: the AI intake record lists Routewise and Routesmith, unverified, among possible suppliers for Halden's route suggestion system (AI-003). If Routewise turns out to be that supplier, retiring it changes an AI system too. Worth one phone call before anyone signs.

The benefit, and how to prove it

  • Measure: Annual savings achieved, each backed by evidence, net of cost the change adds.
  • Baseline in the files: $0 achieved. The tool's range of $250,000 to $355,400 a year is a forecast.
  • Target: Record a saving only after the contract has ended and the spend has stopped.
  • Evidence to keep: The cancellation notice, the last invoice, and the accounts payable record showing the payments stopped. Net out added licenses, such as extra Routesmith seats.
  • Forecast or achieved: Forecast today. It becomes achieved row by row, only as evidence is filed.

Record it in the benefits tracker and measure again after the change.

Renewals: two licenses lapsed and nobody decided

Two licenses were already past expiry on the check date, by 154 and 244 days. The files cannot say who should have decided or by when, because they hold no owner and no notice period.

The situation

Finance asks, before the next budget, what is renewing and when. The inputs are the license file (60 licenses with entitlements, an expiry date and a checked-on date of 2026-09-01) and the inventory with annual cost and users for each application.

What the records showed

58 licenses expire on 2027-06-30, 302 days after the check date, and none fall in the next 180 days. Two have already lapsed: LIC-017 for Meetrix video conferencing (expired 2026-03-31, 154 days before the check; 649 users; $49,600 a year) and LIC-044 for Payflow accounts payable automation (expired 2025-12-31, 244 days before; 748 users; $83,400 a year; confidential data). The two applications carry $133,000 a year of recorded cost. The file has no notice period, no auto-renew flag and no owner for any license, so no notice deadline can be worked out and nobody can be named to decide. All 58 live licenses on one date also means most of the portfolio comes due together.

What the tool did

The rationalization tool checked expiry against the checked-on date and listed renewals due within 180 days with their window, owner and notice deadline wherever the data had them. It put LIC-017 and LIC-044 in the past-due group, left every notice deadline blank, and marked each one Unverified instead of guessing a notice period. It asked for the missing fields to go into the renewal calendar.

The decisions that stayed with people

  • Decision needed: the governance lead names an owner for each application, starting with the two lapsed ones.
  • Decision needed: each owner confirms whether LIC-017 and LIC-044 renewed, lapsed or are in a grace period, then chooses renew, reduce or cancel.
  • Decision needed: procurement supplies the notice period and the auto-renew terms from the contracts, and the finance partner checks the cost.

What a real team would still check

Whether the expiry date is the contract end or the end of a license term. Whether a renewal was paid outside this system, which the invoices would show. Whether the users column matches licensed seats; Payflow's entitlements do not obviously match its 748 users, and that stays an open question. The rationalization case above treats both as worth renewing; this case is about the missed decision, not the value.

The benefit, and how to prove it

  • Measure: Licenses past expiry with no recorded decision.
  • Baseline in the files: 2 of 60 on 2026-09-01 (LIC-017 and LIC-044).
  • Target: 0, and every renewal due in 180 days has an owner, a notice deadline and a decision recorded before that deadline.
  • Evidence to keep: The renewal calendar rows with owner, notice deadline, decision, approver and date; and the contract notice terms.
  • Forecast or achieved: A risk benefit. Any spend avoided by deciding on time is Not found in the files, so none is claimed; add it only when you can show it.

Record it in the benefits tracker and measure again after the change.

Retirement: two devices with no wipe record

Eight of ten retired devices have a wipe confirmation. Two do not: an Apple mobile retired in 2023 and a Dell desktop retired in 2025. The records cannot say whether they were wiped, only that nobody can prove it.

The situation

Security wants assurance before a hardware audit. The input is the device file: 60 devices with type, owner, deployment and retirement dates, wipe confirmation, cost, and a checked-on date of 2026-09-01.

What the records showed

50 devices are active and 10 are retired. DEV-011, an Apple mobile deployed 2021-08-28 and retired 2023-02-19 (1,290 days before the check; $703), and DEV-042, a Dell desktop deployed 2023-07-19 and retired 2025-01-09 (600 days before; $966), both show no wipe confirmation. Their owners, EMP-064 and EMP-067, are still active employees, so someone can be asked. The file does not say what data either device held or where it is now.

What the tool did

The hardware lifecycle tool flagged both devices, kept their disposal open instead of closing it out, and recommended a wipe or certified-destruction method matched to each type. It reminded the team that encryption is a safeguard, not a replacement for wiping. The retirement sign-off checklist then asks IT, Finance, HR, Procurement and Legal each to confirm their part.

The decisions that stayed with people

  • Decision needed: the hardware asset manager finds the devices and chooses wipe or certified destruction for each.
  • Decision needed: the asset owner and the data owner say what data each held, and Legal decides whether anything needs further handling.
  • Decision needed: Finance confirms the write-down and the final cost are recorded.

What a real team would still check

Whether the devices still exist; the file has no location. Whether a wipe happened but was never recorded, since a no could mean not done or not written down. Whether certificates exist for the eight confirmed devices. And what the two owners remember, years later.

The benefit, and how to prove it

  • Measure: Retired devices with wipe confirmation.
  • Baseline in the files: 8 of 10 (80%) on 2026-09-01.
  • Target: 10 of 10, with a certificate or wipe record filed for each, before any device is closed out.
  • Evidence to keep: The wipe or destruction certificate for each device, matched to its device ID, and the retirement sign-off checklist with each function's confirmation.
  • Forecast or achieved: A risk benefit. No money figure is claimed. The benefit is that every retired device can be shown to be clean.

Record it in the benefits tracker and measure again after the change.

As-Is and To-Be: a program plan from the demo data

Scored against evidence, Halden's application management program sits at level 1 on nine of eleven dimensions and level 0 on two. A target of level 2 everywhere gives 13 points of gap and a first-quarter list of three.

The situation

A new governance lead has to give the sponsor a plan. Halden's own maturity answers claim scores of 3 for governance and inventory and 4 for vendor and cost management, with no evidence recorded beside any of them. The lead scores the program again, using the demo files as the evidence.

What the records showed

The draft policy names no accountable role and sets no review cycle. The inventory has no owner column. The license file has no notice or auto-renew fields, and 2 of 60 licenses have expired. 2 of 10 retired devices have no wipe confirmation. The draft council charter names no sponsor. Each of these is a fact in the files, not a judgment.

What the tool did

The self-assessment applies one rule: if you cannot show the evidence for a level, score one level lower. Policy, owners, inventory, intake, licenses, cost, risk, retirement and communication score 1; lifecycle review and measures score 0. Setting the target at 2 for every dimension, with a reason, gives a gap of 13 points. The plan puts policy, renewals and retirement first, due 2026-12-31; owners, inventory, cost and AI risk next, due 2027-03-31; and intake, lifecycle, measures and communication last, due 2027-06-30.

The decisions that stayed with people

  • Decision needed: the sponsor chooses which gaps to fund first. The plan recommends three.
  • Decision needed: whether level 2 is the right target for each dimension, given the risk and the cost.
  • Decision needed: who owns each gap, named by role.

What a real team would still check

These scores come from reading files, not from talking to the people who do the work, so a real team would redo them together. The scale is this kit's own and is not the same as the maturity check's scale. And a target is a choice, not a fact.

The benefit, and how to prove it

  • Measure: Gap points across the 11 dimensions: target level minus current level.
  • Baseline in the files: 13 points on 2026-09-01 (two dimensions at 0, nine at 1, all targeted at 2).
  • Target: 0 by 2027-06-30, re-scored with evidence for each level, not by self-rating.
  • Evidence to keep: The evidence for each level you claim: the approved policy, the owner field, the renewal calendar, the filed wipe records, the first leadership report.
  • Forecast or achieved: A risk and effort measure. It shows movement, not money. Pair it with the renewal, retirement and savings measures above for money and risk figures.

Record it in the benefits tracker and measure again after the change.
